    How early settlement figures work

    When you pay off a loan or car finance early in the UK, you don't pay the interest for the time you no longer need the money. The rules for working out what you owe are set by law. Here's how the figure is calculated, what lenders can add, and how to get yours.

    • Updated 28 November 2026

    The short version

    • You can repay a regulated loan, hire purchase or PCP agreement early, in full or in part, at any time.
    • Ask the lender for a settlement statement. They must send it within 7 days.
    • The figure is what you owe on a date 28 days after they receive your request: your remaining payments, minus a rebate of the interest you won't now be charged.
    • With more than a year left, the lender can work out the rebate as if you settled one month later, which adds about a month's interest.
    • On agreements from 1 February 2011, they may also charge up to 1% (0.5% with a year or less left) if you repay more than £8,000 early in a year.

    How the rebate is calculated

    Under the Consumer Credit (Early Settlement) Regulations 2004, the lender values each payment you still owe at the settlement date, discounting it at the agreement's APR. Payments due soon are worth almost their full amount; payments due years away are worth much less. The total is your settlement figure, and the difference from your remaining payments is your rebate.

    This is the same as the balance you'd owe now if interest were charged at the APR, plus interest up to the settlement date. It's why settling early saves most at the start of a loan, when most of your payments are still interest.

    Worked example

    You borrowed £15,000 at 9.9% APR over 5 years and have made 24 payments of £314.86. You still owe 36 payments, £11,335.01 in total.

    • Settling 28 days after you ask: about £9,904.02, so you save £1,430.99.
    • With the one-month deferral: about £9,979.95.
    • Plus the maximum 1% compensation, because over £8,000 is repaid with more than a year left: about £99.04.
    • Worst case: about £10,078.99, still saving £1,256.02.

    The extra month's interest (the “58 days”)

    Regulation 6 lets the lender defer the settlement date used for the rebate by one month (or 30 days) when more than a year of the agreement is left. Combined with the 28-day notice period, this is why people talk about lenders charging “up to 58 days' interest”. With a year or less left, there's no deferral.

    Early repayment compensation

    For agreements made on or after 1 February 2011, section 95A of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 allows the lender to claim a fair, objectively justified cost of early repayment, only when more than £8,000 is repaid early in any 12 months. It's capped at 1% of the amount repaid if more than a year is left, 0.5% if a year or less is left, and never more than the interest you would have paid. Many lenders don't charge it. Mortgages and overdrafts have different rules.

    Part repayments

    You can also repay part of the loan early. The lender should either shorten the term or lower your payments; ask which. Overpaying regularly saves interest too: see the loan repayment calculator.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long is an early settlement figure valid?

    The statement gives the amount to pay to settle on a date 28 days after the lender received your request. Pay by that date to clear the agreement for the amount shown.

    Can a lender refuse early settlement?

    No. For regulated consumer credit, you have a legal right to repay early, in full or in part.

    Why is my settlement figure more than the balance on my statement?

    It includes interest up to the settlement date, and sometimes an extra month's interest or early repayment compensation.

    Does settling early affect my credit score?

    Settling in full is recorded as a settled account, not a default. Closing the account can change your credit mix slightly.
